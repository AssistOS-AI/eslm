# Probabilistic Scoring and Generation

## 1. Why probability is necessary

A system that only parses and answers questions is not comparable to a causal language model. TinyStories models are trained by next-token prediction and can rank or generate arbitrary continuations. StoryCircuit therefore requires a normalized token-level interface even though its internal state is symbolic.

## 2. Tokenizer-independent and tokenizer-dependent metrics

The primary cross-model information measure is bits per UTF-8 byte or character because it is not distorted by different tokenizers. For direct comparison with official checkpoints, the evaluator also uses the same external tokenizer and reports token NLL and perplexity.

The model exposes prefix scores. Token log probability is computed incrementally using exactly the same token boundaries as the baseline. Any normalization approximation must be reported.

## 3. Mixture of normalized experts

The preferred model is a mixture, not an unnormalized product:

```text
P(t | h) = sum_k gate_k(h) P_k(t | state_k(h))
```

Each expert distribution sums to one, and gates are nonnegative and sum to one. Experts include:

- byte or character backoff;
- word and morpheme n-gram;
- weighted construction grammar;
- discourse and coreference expectation;
- world-state semantic compatibility;
- narrative plan and event transition;
- realization template state.

The lexical expert ensures full support. Symbolic experts may assign sparse distributions and abstain, in which case their gate mass is redistributed according to a declared policy.

## 4. Grammar expert

The grammar expert maintains an incremental chart or construction automaton. Given a prefix, it predicts tokens that can continue active constructions. Production weights are estimated from corpus counts with smoothing and optionally EM over ambiguous parses.

The grammar expert must not hard-mask every unrecognized token during evaluation; otherwise parser gaps become infinite penalties. Instead it reserves probability for escape productions that return control to lexical backoff.

## 5. Semantic experts

World and narrative experts usually operate at word, phrase, or sentence boundaries. Their distributions are projected onto tokenizer tokens through a trie of candidate lexical realizations. For example, when the planner predicts a transfer event, the realization expert may allocate mass to “gave”, “handed”, or a learned paraphrase, conditioned on tense and arguments.

At an incomplete word, lexical and grammar experts dominate. At a completed clause, semantic experts can shift mass toward continuations compatible with current state and goals.

## 6. Gate calibration

Fixed gates are sufficient for the first baseline. More advanced gates are learned on development data using convex optimization or a small transparent model. The gate features may include parser confidence, sentence position, active construction depth, number of unresolved references, and narrative-plan confidence.

A neural gate places the run in S1 but does not invalidate symbolic execution. Its parameter count and ablations must be reported.

## 7. Story scoring

In addition to token likelihood, the evaluator records structural scores:

```text
parse probability
reference probability
world-transition probability
narrative-schema probability
realization probability
constraint penalties
```

This supports ending selection and error explanation. A candidate ending that is lexically common but violates possession or character identity can be rejected by world-state score.

## 8. Hierarchical generation

Generation proceeds through levels.

### Contract extraction

The prompt is parsed into required entities, words, setting, theme, style, length, and outcome constraints. Unclear constraints remain unresolved rather than invented.

### Narrative planning

A planner samples or searches a story schema and fills roles. It generates events while simulating state transitions. Hard constraints are checked during search; soft constraints contribute to score.

### Discourse planning

The model chooses sentence grouping, reference forms, dialogue placement, information order, and paragraph boundaries.

### Surface realization

Weighted constructions generate text. Beam search retains alternatives. The lexical backoff can fill low-level choices but cannot introduce new semantic events without a corresponding plan node unless free-elaboration mode is enabled.

### Verification and repair

The generated story is reparsed. The verifier checks entity identity, temporal order, state invariants, required prompt elements, grammar diagnostics, and plan realization. Failed candidates are repaired or discarded.

## 9. Creativity

A symbolic system can be diverse without unconstrained randomness. Diversity is generated by sampling over cast, settings, goals, obstacles, schemas, event variants, and realizations. Novelty is measured by graph and text distance from training stories. The planner can penalize overly frequent combinations while retaining validity.

The system should report whether novelty arose at the semantic-plan level or only through surface paraphrase.

## 10. Decoding compatibility

The LM protocol supports temperature, top-k, top-p, and deterministic seeds. Since symbolic planning and token generation operate at different levels, the model also exposes hierarchical controls:

```text
plan_temperature
realization_temperature
schema_top_k
max_plan_depth
repair_budget
```

For fair causal-LM comparison, standard token-level controls are used in the main benchmark. Hierarchical controls are reported in a separate generation study.

## 11. Perplexity caveats

Perplexity rewards surface prediction and may undervalue a model that deliberately abstracts lexical variation. It is still necessary because the claim concerns language modeling. Results must be accompanied by bits per byte, parse coverage, story-level structural score, and targeted capabilities.

A symbolic model that obtains excellent reasoning but extremely poor likelihood should be described as a narrative reasoner, not as a competitive language model.
